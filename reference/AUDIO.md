# reference/AUDIO.md — how the reference game does sound

Ripped facts, not design. `rust-systems.txt` answers *what systems exist*,
`SPAWN.md` answers *how the world gets placed*; this file answers **how the
reference game decides what a player hears**, because we were about to build
that and had no evidence at all.

Dated 2026-08-06. §9 is the part that changes what we build.

## 0 · Provenance — read this first

**Source: the developer's own public devblogs, plus the shipped convar list.**
That is a *weaker* source than `rust-systems.txt` (regenerable MIT build data)
and a *cleaner* one than `SPAWN.md` (decompiled proprietary code): nothing
here was decompiled, disassembled or extracted. Every claim below is either a
sentence a developer published about their own work or a convar name and
default that the game prints to any player who types `find audio` in its own
console.

Two consequences:

- **Nothing here is a class name, a method signature or an algorithm read off
  a binary.** Where a mechanism is described it is described from the outside,
  in our own notation, from what a developer said it does.
- **The dates matter.** Most of this is 2015–2017 devblog work, and the audio
  system has had a decade since. Read it as "the shape the system settled
  into", not as "what ships today". Where a fact is a convar and default it is
  current-ish, because the convar list is regenerated by players.

Nothing here ships. No sample, no name, no asset.

## 1 · The engine is Unity's own mixer, and that is a choice they made twice

The reference runs on Unity and uses **Unity's built-in audio**, not FMOD or
Wwise. The devblog that matters is the one where they adopt **Unity 5's audio
mixer**, and what they say they got from it is the whole architecture in one
sentence: groups you can categorize sounds into and balance separately,
**snapshots** that store a whole mixer state, and **fades between snapshots**
— the examples given are making caves echoey and fading music down when
somebody speaks.

So the order of construction was:

1. Groups (buses) — categorize and balance.
2. Snapshots — a named mixer state.
3. Crossfades between snapshots — a *state machine over the mix*.
4. Only then: occlusion, reverb zones, and the rest.

That ordering is the single most useful thing in this document. **A bus
structure is what makes a mix arguable; an occlusion pass on an unmixed game
is a filter on noise.**

## 2 · The budget is a convar, and it is 0.3 milliseconds

From the shipped convar list:

| convar | default | what it is |
|---|---|---|
| `audio.master` | 1 | master volume, 0..1 |
| `audio.game` | 1 | the game bus |
| `audio.ambience` | True | the ambience system, on/off |
| `audio.musicvolume` | 0.2 | in-game music |
| `audio.menumusicvolume` | 0.2 | menu music |
| `audio.voices` | 1 | voice chat |
| `audio.speakers` | 2 | speaker configuration |
| **`audio.framebudget`** | **0.3** | **max milliseconds per frame for sound updates** |
| `audio.occlusion` | (see §7) | sound occlusion, added disabled |
| `audio.advancedocclusion` | False | the more expensive occlusion path |
| `music.enabled` | True | music at all |
| `music.songgapmin` | 240 | shortest gap between songs, seconds |
| `music.songgapmax` | 480 | longest gap between songs, seconds |
| `voice.loopback` | False | hear yourself |
| `client.pushtotalk` | True | push-to-talk rather than open mic |

`audio.framebudget` is the one to take seriously. A shipped survival game
puts a **hard per-frame millisecond cap on its own sound system's update
work**, exposes it as a knob, and defaults it to three tenths of a
millisecond. Audio is a hot path there, and it is treated like one.

The volume convars also settle a scale question: **volumes are 0..1 buses,
not per-sound decibel trims**, and the whole mixer a player can reach is
master / game / music / voices, plus an ambience on-off.

## 3 · Ambience is *localized*, and its bug is a performance bug

The reference has a **localized ambience system**: emitters placed in the
world, culled against the listener, crossfaded by distance, as distinct from a
single global bed. Three published facts about it, all from its own
maintenance:

- **The culling was updating too often.** The performance fix was to *reduce
  the update frequency and make the updates more granular* — the culling
  groups were being rebuilt faster than the world was changing.
- **It leaked.** Ambience performance degraded slowly over a session because
  of a leak in the ambience manager's emitter handling; the fix did not raise
  frame rate so much as stop it falling over hours of play.
- **Things get absorbed into it over time.** River sound started as its own
  thing and was later folded into the local ambience system specifically so it
  would "come in/out more smoothly" — the crossfade machinery is the value,
  and a bespoke sound outside it transitions worse.

Also published: transitions between different ambiences and distance
crossfades were explicitly worked on to sound smoother, and the timing of
layers like birds and crickets is tuned separately from the beds themselves.
Localized tree and bush ambience is its own layer, described as having been
"way less hissy and more detailed" after a pass.

## 4 · The mix is a first-class artifact, and there is one EQ decision worth stealing

Two things the audio developer describes doing that are process, not code:

- **One master project.** Source files were consolidated so the whole game's
  audio could be batch-exported and *swept* — "larger more sweeping changes to
  the mix" — rather than tweaked file by file. The mix is edited as a whole.
- **Ambience is EQ-carved to make room.** The published decision: cut ambience
  in the **2–5 kHz** band, so that gunshots and footsteps have space in the
  mix. That is a stated priority order — *the two sounds that decide fights
  own the band that carries them* — and it is a design rule, not a taste.

Also: reverb tails were added to all gunshot sounds, and weapon foley (cloth,
leather, gear movement) was re-recorded and applied consistently across every
weapon, because inconsistent foley between weapons reads as a bug.

## 5 · Footsteps are per-surface, and the variations are recorded

Barefoot footsteps were recorded across **concrete, dirt, sand, grass and
wood**. Item pickup/place sounds are per material class, with more added over
time (bone, sheet metal, and "special" items like guns and explosives called
out as still owed at the time). Eat/drink sounds are custom, with **multiple
variations per action**.

The load-bearing fact is the last one: **variation is bought with a bank**.
Where the reference has five recordings of a grass footstep, anyone without
that bank has to buy variation some other way or ship a machine gun.

## 6 · The bugs it shipped, which are the most useful part

Four published fixes, all of them the shape of thing a first audio pass gets
wrong:

- **Placement effects fired at the world origin** instead of at the socket
  the object was being deployed to. A positional payload with the wrong
  position — exactly `reference/FINDINGS.md` §1's bug class, in audio.
- **Physics sound lag** when destroying loot boxes.
- **Excess DSP usage from sound occlusion on surround setups**, described by
  the developer as needing a temporary fix and a different approach to the
  filtering because of Unity audio constraints. Occlusion is expensive and its
  cost is *configuration-dependent*.
- **Ambience emitter leak** (§3), which cost performance over hours, not
  frames.

## 7 · Occlusion arrived late, behind a convar, off

The first pass at sound occlusion shipped **disabled behind `audio.occlusion`**
while its author tested it for a week, with players invited to turn it on and
report. A second, more expensive path exists as `audio.advancedocclusion`,
**default False**. Years later a build was still fixing DSP cost from it on
surround setups (§6). Reverb zones exist and have had at least one
optimization pass.

The lesson is the gating, not the algorithm: **a whole-game DSP change went
out off by default, in a game with a decade of audio work already in it.**

## 8 · Music is a gap-and-intensity system, not a soundtrack

The most transferable design in the document, and the cheapest to get wrong.

- **Music is not continuous.** `music.songgapmin` / `songgapmax` default to
  **240 and 480 seconds** — four to eight minutes of silence between songs.
  They were tuned in both directions across devblogs: shortened when music was
  too rare, then lengthened again when it played too often.
- **Themes hold pieces, not tracks.** A theme is a set of short pieces that
  make up a song; a theme is divided into sections (like verses and choruses);
  each section holds clips of differing intensity, scored with slightly
  different instruments so the music keeps moving forward.
- **Each piece carries an intensity level**, and the current in-game intensity
  picks which piece plays next.
- **Pieces end with reverb tails and delays rather than looping cleanly.**
  Stated reason: it lets the system cut from any piece to any other piece in
  any order without stopping playback and without fading. The tail covers the
  join.
- **Intensity is about half musical and half events.** Published event
  weights, in order: a weapon equipped or a helicopter around is a *small*
  bump; a bullet passing your head is a *bigger* bump; taking damage is the
  *biggest*. If nothing happens during a section, intensity drops at the end of
  that section, so it decays back to zero at musical boundaries rather than on
  a timer.

That last mechanism is the good one: **intensity changes only at section
boundaries, so the music never lurches.**

## 9 · What it means for us

Owned by `crate::sound` and `RENDER.md`'s sibling; this section is why those
look the way they do.

1. **Buses before effects, and we are on the same first step.** §1's ordering
   is the reference's own, twice over. `sound::Bus` is two groups (game,
   ambience) with a master over them, mirroring `audio.game` / `audio.ambience`
   / `audio.master`. Snapshots (§1's step 2) are not built and are the natural
   next thing, not occlusion.
2. **The frame budget is a wall, and ours is in the unit we can assert.** §2's
   `audio.framebudget 0.3` is a millisecond cap; a pure module cannot measure
   milliseconds, so ours bounds work items instead — `STARTS_PER_FRAME`,
   `VOICE_CAP`, `CUE_QUEUE_CAP`, each with a stated overflow policy, which is
   `CLAUDE.md` wall 4 anyway. **A millisecond probe is still owed** and would
   be the honest version of the same wall.
3. **Localized ambience is the shape to grow into, not to start with.** §3
   says the machinery's value is the crossfade and that its two published bugs
   were *cull frequency* and *a leak* — both properties of having many
   emitters. Ours is one looping bed whose gain reads the drawn prop density
   around the eye: it answers to the world, it cannot leak an emitter set, and
   it costs a query length. Emitters are a later slice that arrives with a
   cull budget, not without one.
4. **The 2–5 kHz rule is a design rule and we have not paid it.** §4 is a
   stated priority order — footsteps and combat own that band. We have no EQ
   and no ducking at all; the bed is simply quieter. Writing that down is
   honest, and `ART.md` having no audio section is the deeper gap.
5. **Per-surface footsteps, but our surfaces come from the terrain.** §5's
   five recorded surfaces are our four splat channels plus water, and the
   difference is that `terrain::splat` already answers "what is the ground
   made of here" as a pure function the renderer blends by — so the sound
   cannot disagree with the picture. Their variation comes from a bank
   (§5); ours comes from a per-start playback-rate jitter, because we have no
   bank. That is a substitution, not an equal.
6. **§6's origin bug is refused rather than reproduced.** A positional cue
   with no position is *dropped and counted* by the mixer rather than played
   at (0,0,0), and `tests/sound.rs` asserts it. It is the cheapest possible
   answer to a bug class this repo has already been bitten by conceptually
   (`FINDINGS.md` §1) and it costs one branch.
7. **Occlusion is not built, and §7 is the licence for that.** The reference
   gated its first pass behind a convar, off, with a decade of audio behind
   it, and was still paying for it years later. We have no walls in the sim's
   audio-facing model at all — no geometry query a client could ask "is there
   a wall between me and this" without either raycasting the render meshes or
   asking the sim, and the second is the one that would be correct. It is a
   slice with a prerequisite, not a tuning pass.
8. **Music is the highest-value unbuilt thing here, and it is nearly free.**
   §8 is a complete design: a gap timer in minutes, themes of sectioned
   pieces, an intensity scalar bumped by events we already have as integer
   codes (`EV_HIT`, `EV_HEALTH`, a weapon selected), and transitions that
   happen only at section boundaries. Every input exists. What does not exist
   is the music, and a generated bank (`sound/synth.rs`) can make tones but
   cannot make a theme. **That is the honest blocker and it is a content
   blocker, not an engineering one.**
9. **What the reference does that we should NOT copy yet**: reverb zones,
   surround-speaker configuration, voice chat, and per-weapon foley
   consistency — all of them are polish on systems we do not have.
